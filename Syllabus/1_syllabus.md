# **2 Course Description and Objectives**


**2.1** **Course Description**


This course allows students to apply theories, concepts and methodologies taught in various
integrated data science and economics courses to an pre-approved self-directed project. Students
may, for example, work on projects that deepen their understanding of certain aspects of digital
currencies, FinTech or the digital economy, or that involve data-driven problem-solving or a detailed
case study within a practical setting in economics, finance, public policy or society. Each project
culminates in a written report detailing motivation, approach, methods and findings.


**2.2** **Course Objectives**


Upon completion this course, students will be able to:


  - Showcase the knowledge and analytical skills that they have acquired in other data science
and economics courses.


  - Demonstrate an awareness of data-scientific problems of practical interest in economics,
finance, public policy and society.


  - Develop independent research and problem-solving skills in emerging or rapidly developing
technologies or applications.


**2.3** **Prerequisite**


  - DSE3101 Practical Data Science for Economics AND


  - EC3304 Econometrics II

# **3 Course Structure and Policies**


**3.1** **Course Structure**


This course provides students with the opportunity to undertake a self-directed, instructorapproved project in one of three domains: FinTech, digital currencies, or the digital economy.


From Weeks 1 to 6, students will first self-select a domain of interest. Within each domain,
students will be randomly assigned to teams. Each team will then select a group project topic from
a list provided by the coordinator. To encourage early planning and steady progress, each group
must submit a short written proposal by Tuesday of Week 3. This Phase 1 Proposal should explain
the project’s motivation, identify the key literature, provide a basic work plan, and outline each
member’s responsibilities.


Before the Week 6 presentations, each student will be assigned another group’s presentation
to review. To help the assigned discussants prepare thoughtful questions and feedback, each
group must submit its presentation slides by Sunday of Week 6. On Tuesday of Week 6, groups
will present their initial findings and conceptual framework, and each discussant must submit a
written review report by the following Tuesday.


From Weeks 7 to 12, teams will apply their proposed methodologies to real-world data. To
ensure the research remains on track, each group must submit a Phase 2 Proposal by Tuesday of
Week 7. This updated proposal should refine the research question, if necessary, finalize the data
sources and methodology, and provide a more detailed plan for completing the project.


To help the assigned discussants prepare thoughtful questions and feedback, each group must
submit its presentation slides by Sunday of Week 12. On Tuesday of Week 12, groups will deliver
their final presentations. Each group must then submit its written report by Friday of Week 12.
Each discussant must submit a final review report on the assigned group’s written report by Friday
of Week 13.

**Proposals.** Two (2) group project proposals together account for 10% of the course grade, with 5%
allocated to each.


1. The **Phase 1 Proposal**, due in Week 3, accounts for 5% of the course grade. This short
written document should clearly articulate the project’s motivation, list key literature,
outline each member’s responsibilities, and provide a basic work plan. It serves to
ensure the group starts the project with a clear direction and realistic goals. This sets
the foundation for the mid-term presentation.


2. The **Phase 2 Proposal**, due in Week 7, accounts for 5%. This updated proposal should
justify the chosen dataset, define the research questions and methods, describe expected
outcomes, assign tasks, and lay out a timeline for completion.


**Presentations.** Two (2) group project presentations constitute 25% of the final grade, with 10%
allocated to Mid-Term Group Presentation and 15% the Final Group Presentation.


1. The **Mid-Term Group Presentation**, delivered in Week 6, contributes 10% to the final
grade. Each group will present their understanding of the relevant literature and the

conceptual framework underpinning their project. The clarity, coherence, and relevance
of the presentation will be key grading criteria.


2. The **Final Group Presentation**, held in Week 12, is worth 15% of the grade. The
presentation should demonstrate the application of theoretical and empirical tools to
real-world data, provide insightful interpretation of findings, and communicate results

**Participation as Discussants.** Serving as a discussant is an integral part of this course and contributes 10% to the final grade. As discussants, students are expected to engage actively

**Final Report.** The Final Group Report, due after the Week 12 presentation, makes up 25% of
the course grade. This report should provide a detailed and rigorous discussion of the
project, including motivation, literature, data description, empirical strategy, findings, and
conclusions.


**Review Reports.** Each student is required to submit two review reports — one following the midterm group presentation and another after the final group project. These reviews together
account for 30% of the course grade.


1. The **Mid-Term Review Report on a Peer Group Presentation**, due in Week 7, is worth
10%. Each student will be assigned to a presentation and serve as a discussant. The
report should succinctly summarize the main findings of the assigned presentation and
provide constructive feedback on the clarity, coherence, and relevance of the content.


2. The **Final Review Report on a Peer Group Project**, due in Week 13, contributes 20% to
the final grade. In this assignment, each student will be assigned a group project report
from another domain. The report should summarize the key ideas and findings of the
project and offer a thoughtful critique of its research design, data usage, methodology,
and conclusions. Students are expected to assess both the technical execution and the
clarity of communication.


**Peer Evaluation of Group Contributions.** While the group components are graded based on
the quality of the group’s work, each student’s final score for the group components may
be adjusted to reflect their individual contribution. At the end of the semester, all students
will complete a confidential peer evaluation of their teammates’ contributions. If at least
half of a student’s teammates report that the student did not contribute adequately to the
group project, the student’s score for the group assessment components will be adjusted. To
ensure fairness, any claim of inadequate contribution must be supported by evidence, such
as communication records, version history, meeting attendance, task allocation records, or
other relevant documentation.


Your grade will be based on your performance over the semester. The previous six paragraphs

**Academic Honesty** The University is committed to nurturing an environment conducive for the
exchange of ideas, advancement of knowledge and intellectual development. Academic
honesty and integrity are essential conditions for the pursuit and acquisition of knowledge,
and the University expects each student to maintain and uphold the highest standards of
integrity and academic honesty at all times.


The University takes a strict view of cheating in any form, deceptive fabrication, plagiarism
and violation of intellectual property and copyright laws. Any student who is found to have
engaged in such misconduct will be subject to disciplinary action by the University. Such
misconduct will include, but is not limited to, the misuse of content or language generated
by artificial intelligence (AI) computer programmes.


It is important to note that all students share the responsibility of protecting the academic
standards and reputation of the University. This responsibility can extend beyond each student’s own conduct, and can include reporting incidents of suspected academic dishonesty
through the appropriate channels. Students who have reasonable grounds to suspect academic dishonesty should raise their concerns directly to the relevant Head of Department,
Dean of Faculty, Registrar, Vice Provost, or Provost.


**Plagiarism** Plagiarism is the taking of the work or ideas of another person or entity, including
artificial intelligence (AI), and presenting them as one’s own without proper attribution. This
is a form of academic dishonesty. It undermines fair evaluation of the student’s own learning
and effort, and it also undermines the system of scholarly research and development of ideas.
NUS does not condone plagiarism, regardless of source.


**AI Tools** ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE TOOLS such as ChatGPT are now essentially costless
and do not require special expertise to use. As part of the repertoire of tools available in
the modern world, we understand how you–our students–might be drawn to using them.
It is also likely employers and stakeholders will soon assume that graduates have a basic
competency for using such tools, just like how they now assume that new hires are able
to use calculators, spreadsheets, and word processing software. That said, there are some
things we need you to take to heart.

FIRST, while we are fine with you experimenting with new tools, we are also wary you will
end up taking shortcuts that will disadvantage you in the longer run. Consider, for instance,
the different levels of capability involved in the following three scenarios:


1. Using a tool to generate an output wholesale from inputs.
2. Using a tool to generate intermediate outputs that are then developed into a final output
through further human intervention without the use of the tool.
3. Evaluating the output of a tool to confirm its accuracy, relevance, objectivity, and completeness.


You will need a higher level of capability in yourself to be able to do (2) and (3), as compared
with doing (1), and conversely, functions that only require (1) are at a higher risk of being
completely replaced by AI. The implication is that you are only cheating your future selves
if you go straight to using such tools before learning the actual subject matter. If you, as a
learner, take shortcuts today, you risk becoming first in line to be replaced by bots!


Since we target learning outcomes that hone higher-level capabilities, and we want our
students to demonstrate originality, voice, intellectual engagement with content, rigor, and
contribution to discourse, we will often need you to produce unaided work as part of your
assessment. This is no different from how, even though we expect you to use calculators and
spreadsheets, we still want you to learn the underlying math and computational processes.
And even though we are fine with you using search engines, spelling and grammar checkers,
or for that matter, lexicons and thesauri, we still want you to be able to independently
organize your thoughts and produce your own compositions–so that you can assess the tools’
deliverances. SECOND, note that normal academic rules continue to apply. As provided in
the Code of Student Conduct:


_The University takes a strict view of cheating in any form, deceptive fabrication, plagiarism and_
_violation of intellectual property and copyright laws. Any student who is found to have engaged in_
_such misconduct will be subject to disciplinary action by the University. In this regard, representing_
_an AI’s output as your own work is plagiarism. (See “Guidelines on the Use of AI Tools for Academic_
_Work”.)_


THIRD, do be aware that the tools typically require you to sign up for an account and
accept various terms and conditions. We encourage you to be very clear about what you are
accepting if you sign up for an account. You should also be aware that there are lawsuits
underway challenging the intellectual property provenance of many of these tools. If they
are ruled illegal (while this is unlikely, it is not impossible), there may be implications for
your use as well. And finally, don’t forget that like all online tools, platforms can sometimes
go down without warning, or are under such high load that their responses become too slow
for use. It is thus unwise to assume that you will always have access.
